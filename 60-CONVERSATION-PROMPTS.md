# 60 ways to ask about a child's day

None of them is *"How was school?"*

That question fails for three reasons: it asks a child to summarize seven hours,
it asks for a verdict rather than a memory, and it can be closed with one word.
Every prompt below is specific, retrieves a moment, and is hard to answer with "fine".

Each carries the reason it works — the pattern matters more than the wording, and a
parent who understands the pattern starts inventing their own.

**One rule for all of them: ask one question, then be quiet longer than feels comfortable.**


## Anchors — the day itself

**1. “What was the funniest thing that happened today?”**  
*Asks for a story instead of a verdict — and stories are where the actual information is.*  
Then: “Who else thought it was funny?”

**2. “What was the hardest part of today?”**  
*Gives permission for the day to have been hard, which is usually what "fine" is covering.*  
Then: “Did it get easier, or did it just end?”

**3. “Rate today out of ten — and tell me what the missing points were.”**  
*The number is a warm-up. The missing points are the real question, and children answer it precisely.*  
Then: “What would have made it a ten?”

**4. “What do you know now that you didn't know this morning?”**  
*Reframes learning as something that happened to them, not something they were tested on.*  
Then: “Who told you, or did you work it out?”

**5. “If you only got to tell me one thing about today, what would it be?”**  
*Forces a choice, which surfaces what actually mattered instead of what happened first.*  
Then: “Why that one?”

**6. “What was the loudest part of your day?”**  
*A sensory question is easy to answer and almost always leads somewhere.*  
Then: “Were you part of the loud, or near it?”

**7. “Did today go fast or slow?”**  
*Pace is how children experience a day. The answer tells you more than a list of subjects would.*  
Then: “Which part dragged?”

**8. “What's the first thing you'd change about today?”**  
*Invites a small complaint, which is often the door to the real thing.*  
Then: “Could you change it tomorrow?”

**9. “Where were you standing when the best part of today happened?”**  
*Anchoring to a place reliably retrieves a memory that "what happened" doesn't.*  
Then: “Who was with you?”

**10. “What's something that happened today that you didn't expect?”**  
*Surprise is memorable, so this is one of the easiest questions to answer honestly.*  
Then: “Was it a good surprise?”


## People

**11. “Who did you sit with at lunch?”**  
*The most reliable social question there is — one factual answer, no judgment in it.*  
Then: “Is that who you usually sit with?”

**12. “Who did you help today?”**  
*Assumes competence and generosity, and gives a child a role to describe rather than a grade.*  
Then: “Did they figure it out?”

**13. “Who helped you today?”**  
*Normalizes needing help by asking about it as an ordinary event.*  
Then: “Did you ask, or did they notice?”

**14. “Who made you laugh?”**  
*Finds the person your child actually enjoys, which is rarely the one they name as a "best friend".*  
Then: “What did they do?”

**15. “Did anyone seem like they were having a hard day?”**  
*Practises noticing other people, and often reveals something about your own child's day sideways.*  
Then: “Did anybody check on them?”

**16. “Was there anyone new to talk to today?”**  
*Low-stakes way into the social map without asking about friendships directly.*  
Then: “Would you talk to them again?”

**17. “Who would you want on your team for absolutely anything?”**  
*Gets at trust rather than popularity, and children answer it fast.*  
Then: “What makes them good to have?”

**18. “Did anybody surprise you today?”**  
*Opens room for a good surprise or a bad one without steering toward either.*  
Then: “Had they done that before?”

**19. “Who did you say something nice to?”**  
*Asks about kindness as an action they took, not a quality they have.*  
Then: “How did they react?”

**20. “If your class got a team name today, what should it be?”**  
*A playful question that quietly reports the mood of the whole room.*  
Then: “What would last week's have been?”


## Effort and mistakes

**21. “What's something you tried even though you might get it wrong?”**  
*Names the risk rather than the result, so the brave part is what gets remembered.*  
Then: “What made you decide to try it anyway?”

**22. “What did you get wrong today, and what happened next?”**  
*The second half is the whole question — it treats a mistake as a thing with an afterwards.*  
Then: “Would you do the same next time?”

**23. “Did you have to try anything twice?”**  
*Asks about the restart, which is the behaviour you actually want repeated.*  
Then: “Was the second try different?”

**24. “What do you do first when you get stuck?”**  
*Surfaces the strategy in their own words, which is what makes it repeatable.*  
Then: “Does it usually work?”

**25. “What's something that was hard at the start and easier by the end?”**  
*Builds the idea that difficulty is a stage, not a verdict.*  
Then: “When did it start feeling easier?”

**26. “What are you better at than you were last week?”**  
*Short timescales make progress visible to a child in a way "this year" never does.*  
Then: “How can you tell?”

**27. “Was there a moment you nearly gave up but didn't?”**  
*Invites them to describe persistence as a decision they made.*  
Then: “What kept you going?”

**28. “What did you need help with today?”**  
*Frames asking for help as normal and expected rather than as a failure.*  
Then: “Was it easy to ask?”

**29. “Did you ask a question out loud today?”**  
*Speaking up in front of peers is a bigger risk than adults remember.*  
Then: “Was anyone else wondering the same thing?”

**30. “What was the bravest thing you did today?”**  
*Lets a child define bravery for themselves, which is usually smaller and truer than you'd guess.*  
Then: “Was anyone watching?”


## Curiosity and learning

**31. “Tell me one thing that made you go "huh".”**  
*Asks for curiosity rather than achievement, and children answer it far more often than adults expect.*  
Then: “Do you want to know more about it?”

**32. “What did you wonder about today?”**  
*Signals that questions are the interesting part, not the answers.*  
Then: “How could we find out?”

**33. “Teach me one thing you learned today.”**  
*Explaining is the step that turns something remembered into something understood.*  
Then: “What part is hard to explain?”

**34. “What's something you learned that seems weird to you?”**  
*"Weird" gives a child permission to find school strange, which is often where real thinking starts.*  
Then: “Weird good or weird confusing?”

**35. “If you could ask your teacher one thing, what would it be?”**  
*Reveals the question they didn't feel able to ask in the room.*  
Then: “Could you ask tomorrow?”

**36. “What's something you want to know more about?”**  
*Points at the interest worth feeding, which is rarely the subject they're graded best in.*  
Then: “Where could we look?”

**37. “What was the most interesting thing anybody said today?”**  
*Widens the field beyond the lesson to include everything they overheard.*  
Then: “Who said it?”

**38. “Was there a question nobody could answer today?”**  
*An unanswered question is an invitation, and children remember them vividly.*  
Then: “What do you think the answer is?”

**39. “What's something you figured out without being told?”**  
*Independent thinking is invisible unless somebody names it out loud.*  
Then: “How did you work it out?”

**40. “What would you put in a museum about today?”**  
*Concrete and playful, and it forces a choice about what mattered.*  
Then: “What would the label say?”


## Feelings and hard moments

**41. “What part of the day would you skip if you could?”**  
*A child will name a specific twenty minutes long before they can name a feeling.*  
Then: “What happens right before that part?”

**42. “When did you feel most like yourself today?”**  
*Finds the conditions where your child is comfortable, which is worth knowing and worth protecting.*  
Then: “Who else was there?”

**43. “Was there anything today you'd want a do-over on?”**  
*Non-accusing, and it works whether or not anything actually happened.*  
Then: “What would you do differently?”

**44. “What made you feel proud today?”**  
*Asks for their own standard rather than yours.*  
Then: “Did anyone notice?”

**45. “Was anything unfair today?”**  
*Fairness is the frame children think in, so this gets straighter answers than "was anything wrong".*  
Then: “What would have been fair?”

**46. “When did you feel calm today?”**  
*Locating calm is more useful than locating stress, because calm can be repeated on purpose.*  
Then: “What made it calm?”

**47. “What annoyed you today?”**  
*Small and specific, and it very often opens onto something larger.*  
Then: “Does that happen a lot?”

**48. “Did anything worry you today?”**  
*Asked plainly and without alarm, so a child can answer plainly.*  
Then: “Is it still worrying you now?”

**49. “When did you feel included today?”**  
*Asks about belonging from the positive side, which is easier to answer honestly.*  
Then: “Who made that happen?”

**50. “Was there a moment you felt left out?”**  
*Direct, but only ask it once you've asked the one above — order matters.*  
Then: “What would have helped?”


## Sideways and imaginative

**51. “If you were the teacher tomorrow, what would you change?”**  
*Sideways route into how school actually feels, with none of the pressure of being asked directly.*  
Then: “What would you keep exactly the same?”

**52. “If today were a weather report, what would it say?”**  
*Gives a child a way to describe a mood without needing the vocabulary for it.*  
Then: “What's the forecast for tomorrow?”

**53. “What colour was today?”**  
*Sounds like a game, works like a mood check, and gets answered instantly.*  
Then: “What colour was yesterday?”

**54. “If today were a movie, what would it be called?”**  
*A title forces a summary, and children summarize far more honestly in play than on request.*  
Then: “Who's the main character?”

**55. “What should somebody invent to make school better?”**  
*The invention always points at the actual problem.*  
Then: “Who would use it most?”

**56. “If you could swap jobs with anyone at school for a day, who?”**  
*Reveals who they admire, envy, or don't understand — all useful.*  
Then: “What would you do first?”

**57. “What's one school rule you'd keep exactly as it is?”**  
*Asking what's working is unusual enough that children think properly about it.*  
Then: “What would happen without it?”

**58. “If your day had a soundtrack, what would be playing right now?”**  
*A wind-down question that lands emotional information without asking for it.*  
Then: “What was playing this morning?”


## Looking forward

**59. “What are you looking forward to tomorrow?”**  
*Ends the conversation pointing forwards, which is a good place to leave a child at bedtime.*  
Then: “Is there anything you're not looking forward to?”

**60. “What do you want to do differently tomorrow?”**  
*Turns reflection into a plan, and keeps it small enough to actually happen.*  
Then: “What would help you remember?”
